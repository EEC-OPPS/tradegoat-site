What a Paper Service Ticket Can’t Do

By Travis Evans, Founder of TradeGOAT · 20+ years in the trades

I am not going to tell you the paper service ticket is broken. It is not. It has run service departments for fifty years. The tech fills it out at the unit, the customer signs it, they tear off a copy and the tech keeps one. That is a good system and it is the reason I did not change any part of it.

Our digital service ticket is the exact same ticket. Your form, your fields, your headings, whatever your company already calls things. It is not a new process for your techs to learn. It is your ticket on a phone instead of on a clipboard.

What changes is everything that happens to the ticket after it is signed.

What paper does well

Give paper its due. It works with no signal in a basement. It never runs out of battery. Every tech already knows how to use one, and the customer signing it understands exactly what he is signing. Any replacement that cannot do those things is a downgrade, and plenty of software has tried.

Where paper runs out

It cannot hold a picture. This is the big one. Your tech is looking at a burned contactor, a cracked sheave, water where water should not be. The paper ticket gets a written description and that is the best it can do. Ten words go down about a part that a photograph would have settled instantly, for the customer, for the office, and for you a year later.

It only exists in one place at a time. The office copy is in the truck until the tech brings it in. That is not the tech being lazy, that is just how paper works. Meanwhile the office is billing, answering the customer, and scheduling around work it cannot see yet.

It has to be found again. Every real use of an old ticket, whether that is a warranty question, a repeat failure, a customer disputing a bill, or writing up the repair the tech recommended, starts with somebody digging through a folder for a piece of paper from six weeks ago. Sometimes it turns up. Sometimes it does not, and the work quietly does not get billed.

Somebody has to carry it. A tech running four calls a day has a stack in the truck by Friday. Keeping up with it is real work, it is nobody's favorite part of the job, and it is where tickets get lost, coffee-stained, or handed in three weeks late.

Same ticket, with pictures on it

The digital version does everything the paper one did. The tech fills it out at the unit, both signatures go on the screen right there, and the signed copy sends. Then it does the parts paper cannot.

Pictures attach to the ticket. One tap on the camera, as many as the job needs, and they live on the ticket itself, not in a text thread and not in a phone's camera roll that gets wiped when the phone gets replaced. Before and after on a repair. The nameplate on a part he needs to match. Proof the work was done and proof of the condition he found it in.

That last one matters more than people expect. Photographs end arguments. A customer questioning a bill six weeks later is looking at the same picture the tech took, attached to the ticket he signed.

The part I would want to know: it is your existing ticket. We load the service ticket form your company already uses, with your fields and your wording. Your techs are filling out the same document they filled out last week, and your customers are signing the same thing they have always signed.

The tech and the office are finally looking at the same thing

When the ticket is signed, it is done. It is filed against the right unit and it is in the office. Nobody is carrying a stack around. Nobody is waiting on Friday for a folder of paper.

The tech opens the ticket and sees his notes and his pictures. The office opens the same ticket and sees the same notes and the same pictures. That sounds small until you count how much of the day gets burned on the phone call that exists only because two people are looking at different information, or at no information, because the ticket is still in the truck.

It makes the tech's day easier because he is not managing paperwork he already finished. It makes the office's day easier because the work is billable the day it happened instead of whenever the paper shows up. And it makes the customer conversation easier because whoever answers the phone can pull up the unit and see everything, without asking to call him back.

The repair he found turns into real work

Here is the one that actually makes money, and it is the thing paper is worst at.

Your tech is at a unit for a maintenance call and he sees something that needs to be repaired. It is not today's work. On paper, he writes it in a corner of the ticket and hopes somebody reads it. That ticket rides in the truck, and then it goes into a folder. Two weeks later somebody has to remember it existed, find it, read his handwriting, and figure out what he meant, and if that does not happen, the repair never gets quoted and you never see the job.

On the digital ticket he flags it right there when he is standing in front of it, with a description and what he thinks it needs. It attaches to that unit and that visit. Then the office sees the open items on the unit, checks the ones to quote, and builds a proposal from them, with no hunting for a six-week-old ticket, no deciphering handwriting, and no calling the tech to ask what he meant. It even keeps track of which ones have already been quoted so nothing gets sent twice or forgotten.

That is found work. Every service company has some, and most of it is lost on paper between the truck and the file cabinet.

Straight about where this stands

This is running in the beta now. It is not battle-tested across a thousand shops yet and I am not going to say it is. What I can tell you is what it is built on: the same ticket you already use, because the fastest way to get a tech to stop using software is to hand him somebody else's form.

That is the same reason TradeGOAT takes the proposal you already send instead of asking you to rebuild it. Your paperwork is not the problem. What happens to it after it is signed is the problem.

And once the tickets are in one place, the rest gets easier: pricing a service call the same way every time, and knowing at the end of it whether you actually made money.
