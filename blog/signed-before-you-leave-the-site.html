The Service Ticket Should Be Signed Before You Leave the Site

By Travis Evans, Founder of TradeGOAT · 20+ years in the trades

A service ticket has one right moment, and it is a narrow one. The tech is standing at the equipment. The building rep is there. The work is finished and both of them can still see it. Everything the ticket needs to say is true right now and visible to two people at the same time.

That is when the ticket gets written and that is when it gets signed. Not at the shop that evening, not from notes on Monday. At the unit, on the day, by both people, before the truck leaves the property.

Everything downstream in a service business — the equipment history, the maintenance clock, the invoice, and the answer you give a year later when somebody questions the work — is built on that one document being made at that one moment.

Why the signature is the whole thing

Two signatures on a ticket are not a formality. They are the entire reason the document has weight later.

The tech signs because the record needs an author. Not a login, not whoever happened to be at a keyboard — the person who put hands on the equipment, by name.

The building rep signs because that is the moment agreement exists. He watched the work. He saw the condition it was left in. He is agreeing to what the ticket says while he still has every reason to read it and correct it. A signature obtained then is worth more than a hundred emails obtained after somebody disputes a bill, because at that point you are asking a person to agree to a description of something he can no longer see.

The rule the ticket is built on: the ticket is the log, not the bill. The tech records what happened — findings, work, parts, miles, condition. Rates and charges never appear on the document the customer signs. Pricing is the office’s job, and it happens after.

What the ticket carries off the site

By the time the tech pulls out of the lot, the ticket should already hold all of it:

  1. What was found, what was done, and what it still needs. That last one is the part that turns into your next job, and it is the part that gets left off when the ticket is written from memory.
  2. The unit it was performed on — the actual piece of equipment, not just the address. A building has six of them, and the history has to hang on the right one.
  3. Photos. The failed part, the condition before, the repair after. Proof of work costs about eight seconds on site and is unobtainable the moment you drive away.
  4. Parts and miles, logged as quantities, with no prices on the page.
  5. Both signatures, and the date the work was actually performed.

Then it sends. The customer gets their signed copy and the office gets its copy, from the site, before anyone has driven anywhere. The building rep has the record in his inbox before he is back at his desk, which is roughly when he is going to want it.

And then it becomes money

A finished ticket is not the end of the transaction, it is the start of it. Some of that work is covered by the maintenance contract and some of it is not, and the person best positioned to know which is the tech who just did it. So the ticket carries that call — contract or billable — and billable tickets land on the office’s needs-billing list.

The office applies the company’s rates and turns it into a bill. That separation is deliberate. The tech is not negotiating price in a lobby, and the customer is not signing something with a number on it he did not agree to. He is signing for the work. The number is a conversation with the office, backed by a signed document describing exactly what was done.

This is where service revenue quietly leaks in most shops. Not from bad rates — from repair work that was performed, never written down as billable, and never billed at all. A ticket that leaves the site complete closes that hole.

It is the same discipline as the rest of the job

A signed ticket at the unit is the service-side version of everything else that makes a trade business work: capture it once, capture it right, at the moment it is true. It is the same reason you set a service-call price you can charge every time instead of deciding in the truck, and the same reason you get the change signed before you do the extra work. Do that consistently and you can finally answer the question that matters: did you make money on it.

Twenty years in and four running my own company. The tickets that ever cost me anything were the ones nobody signed.
