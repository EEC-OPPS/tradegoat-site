The Service Ticket Nobody Fills Out in the Truck

By Travis Evans, Founder of TradeGOAT · 20+ years in the trades

Here is how a service ticket actually gets written in a trade business. A tech runs four calls on Thursday. He writes on the back of a work order at the second one, texts a photo from the third, and remembers the fourth. Friday he is on another job all day. Monday morning somebody in the office sits down with his notes, a couple of texts and a phone call, and types up all four.

Every piece of software I have ever used assumes that is not what happens. It assumes the tech stands in the machine room and fills out a form on his phone before he leaves. Some techs do. Most do not, and the ones who do are not doing it on the bad days, which are the days you most need the record.

The two things the software gets wrong

When the office types up Thursday’s work on Monday, two facts get quietly recorded wrong.

The first is the date. The system stamps the day the ticket was typed, not the day the work happened. So your history says you were out there Monday. Four months later you are looking at a callback on the same unit, trying to work out whether this is the same problem you fixed before, and the dates do not line up with anything. The customer remembers Thursday, because Thursday is when a truck was in his parking lot.

The second is the name. The system records whoever was signed in, which is the office. So the ticket says the office manager performed the service. Now pull up a tech’s work for the month, or answer a customer asking who was on his site, or settle a warranty question about who did the install. The record cannot tell you, because it was never asked.

Neither of those is a dramatic failure. Nothing turns red. You just end up with a service history that is subtly, permanently wrong — and service history is the thing you go back to when there is money or liability on the line.

The distinction that fixes it: when the work happened and who did it are different facts from when it was typed and who typed it. A ticket needs all four, and it should never guess two of them from the other two.

What a ticket has to capture

If you are looking at service software right now, or fixing your own paper process, these are the fields I would not compromise on.

  1. The date of service, entered by hand, separate from the entry date. If you cannot set it to last Thursday while typing it on Monday, your history is going to drift and you will not notice for a year.
  2. The technician who performed the work, separate from the user who entered it. Anything else makes per-tech reporting and warranty questions unanswerable.
  3. The unit or the equipment, not just the address. A building can have six pieces of equipment. “Serviced 4400 Westheimer” is not a record, it is a reminder.
  4. What was found, what was done, and what it needs next. The last one is where your next job comes from, and it is the one that gets left off.
  5. Whether it was billed, and against what. A ticket that never connects to an invoice is how service work quietly stops being profitable.

Why the field usually gets locked out

There is a second version of this problem worth naming, because I hit it building our own tool and I doubt we are alone.

Field roles get set up as the most restricted account in the system — sensible, since you do not want pricing and margins on a phone in a customer’s lobby. But restrict it a little too hard and the tech cannot complete a ticket at all. And the way that usually shows up is the worst possible way: the button is right there on his screen, he taps it, and nothing works. No explanation. He tries twice, decides the app is broken, and goes back to writing on the work order. You have now paid for software the field does not use, and you find out months later.

The lesson I took from that is narrow but it matters: a role has to be able to finish the job it exists to do, and when something genuinely is blocked, the screen has to say so in a sentence a person can read. Silent refusal is how good software gets abandoned in the field.

How we handle it

In TradeGOAT there is a Technician role built for the field. It opens on the work — the tickets assigned to that person — and it can complete them, which sounds obvious and is exactly what most tools get wrong.

A ticket records the day the work happened and the tech who did it as their own fields, separate from the day it was typed and whoever was signed in. That is the whole point of the change: the office can write up Thursday’s call on Monday and the record still says Thursday, and still names the tech. The office workflow is not treated as a workaround. It is treated as the normal way service gets documented, because it is.

Techs can also request time off and ask for a schedule change from the same field screen. They ask, the office approves. It is a small thing that removes a surprising number of phone calls.

I will be straight about where this stands, same as always: this is running in the beta right now and it is not battle-tested across a thousand shops yet. What I can tell you is that the date-of-service and technician fields are real fields on the ticket, not a note you type into a description box and hope somebody reads.

It connects to everything downstream

An honest ticket is not paperwork for its own sake. It is the input for the rest of it. Pricing a service call so you actually make money depends on knowing what the last ten calls really took. Knowing whether you made money depends on the work being attached to the job it belongs to. And all of it starts from your own paperwork and your own terms, kept the way you already write them, instead of a form somebody else designed for a business that is not yours.

Twenty years in and four running my own company, the tickets I regret are never the ones written badly. They are the ones written accurately about the wrong day, by the wrong name, because the software never asked.
