Is Paying for Software Worth It, or Should You Build Your Own?

By Travis Evans, Owner of Evans Elevator and Founder of TradeGOAT · 20+ years in the trades

I saw a contractor ask a good question in a business group this week. He’s paying close to $4,000 a year for the software that runs his shop, and with how far AI has come, he’s wondering whether he should build his own instead. He isn’t trying to sell software to anybody. He just wants something that works for his company and his people. Has anyone done it, and was it worth it?

I can answer that one, because we did it. I own an elevator company, and I’ve been in the trades a long time. We weren’t satisfied with anything on the market, so we built our own.

Elevator and escalator work has a lot of moving parts. Every unit in a building has its own history: maintenance visits, repairs, inspections, requests for service, modifications, time cards, electrical drawings and parts orders. Nothing we tried would let us break that history down unit by unit and pull it into one report.

So we tracked it all on different spreadsheets. The information could be found, but the data entry and the time it took to put it together was a real pain. It was a full-time job for somebody, and that somebody was usually me.

Now, at the end of the year, we can hand a property manager or building owner one report. It lists every piece of equipment we service in their building and shows everything that was done to each unit over the year.

That report does more than make the customer happy. It also tells us whether a unit is worth keeping under a maintenance contract.

Units and buildings with a poor record of uptime get looked at hard when the contract comes up for renewal. We can see the total downtime on each unit and the number of repair calls we’ve run on it over the life of the contract. We can also see every time we recommended replacing a part or taking a unit down for repairs, and whether the customer approved it or turned it down, and why.

When you can see all of that in one place, deciding where to put your labor hours gets easy. Sometimes the right call is to spend those hours somewhere else.

Was building it worth it?

For us it wasn’t about saving the software bill. It was a necessity. Our problem was the way the work had to be tracked, and nothing on the market was going to solve it. It isn’t always as simple as staying with one program or switching to another.

People assume a contractor who doesn’t write code has no business building software. What nobody takes into account is that what we needed didn’t exist at any cost. Any company, or any reasonable person, can understand why we’d stop paying for what wasn’t working and build our own.

If you’re asking the same question, start with what you need to see at the end of the year. If the tools you pay for already show you that, keep paying for them. If they don’t, and you’ve got the same pile of spreadsheets we had, that’s the problem worth solving.

That’s why I built TradeGOAT. It runs Evans Elevator every day, and it’s built for any trade where the equipment, the visits and the paperwork pile up.
