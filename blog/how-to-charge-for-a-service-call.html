How to Charge for a Service Call Without Guessing

By Travis Evans, Founder of TradeGOAT · 20+ years in the trades

Big project bids, most guys sweat over. Service calls, most guys wing. And that’s backwards, because you run a lot more service calls than big projects, and the money you leave on the table on each one adds up faster than you’d think.

A service call feels small, so it gets priced from your gut, something like “eh, call it a hundred bucks,” and half the time that hundred bucks doesn’t even cover what it cost you to show up. Here’s how to set a number you can charge every time, without guessing, and actually make money on it.

First, what a service call actually costs you

The reason service-call pricing feels like guesswork is that the real cost is mostly invisible. You see the hour of labor. You don’t see everything stacked underneath it.

  • The trip costs you. That is drive time, fuel and wear on the truck. The clock started when you left the shop, not when you arrived.
  • The labor has to be figured at a real rate. That is not just the wage you pay, it is the wage plus the taxes, insurance, and burden that come with it.
  • Overhead rides along on every call. It covers your phone, your insurance, your software, the shop and the truck payment. Every hour you work has to carry a slice of the cost of being in business at all, and that includes service calls.
  • Materials and parts cost money. Even the small stuff off the truck has a cost, and “I had it on hand” is not the same as free.

Add those up and the number that actually keeps you whole is almost always higher than the one your gut throws out. The gut number ignores the costs it can’t see, and that is the whole problem.

Set a service rate once, and stop re-deciding

The fix isn’t to do this math on every call. You’d never make it out of the driveway. The fix is to do it once and turn it into a rate you can charge on autopilot.

Decide your real cost of putting a person in front of a customer for an hour, which is labor at true cost, with overhead folded in. Add the margin you need to actually profit, not just break even. Now you have a service rate. A lot of contractors run a different rate for service work than they do for project bids, and that’s fine, because service work carries more trip and overhead per hour, so it often should cost more per hour. The point is you set it deliberately, once, instead of re-deciding it in the truck on every call.

Charge for the trip, and set a minimum

Two things almost every profitable service business does, and most strugglers don’t:

Charge for showing up. A trip charge or a service-call fee isn’t nickel-and-diming, it’s covering a real cost that happens before you turn a single screw. The customer called you. Your time getting there is worth money.

Set a minimum. No call should cost you money to run. A minimum charge makes sure the fifteen-minute jobs still cover the trip and the overhead behind them. If that feels aggressive, remember: the alternative is paying customers for the privilege of driving to them.

The quiet margin killer isn’t a low number. It’s a different number every time. The same job should get the same price every time, regardless of your mood.

The real enemy is inconsistency

Here’s what quietly kills margin on service work: it’s not that your number is too low. It’s that it’s different every time. One customer gets the good-mood price, the next gets the it’s-Friday price, and you have no idea across a whole year whether service work is making you money or costing you money, because you never charged the same way twice.

Consistency is the whole game. The same job should get the same number, every time, no matter your mood or how the morning went. That’s not just about profit. It’s what a real business looks like from the outside, too, because a customer who calls you twice and gets two wildly different numbers starts to wonder.
